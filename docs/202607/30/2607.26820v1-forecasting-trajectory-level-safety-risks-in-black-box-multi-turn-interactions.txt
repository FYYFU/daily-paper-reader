Abstract 

As large language models (LLMs) evolve from standalone as-sistants into autonomous agents, ensuring their safety requires shifting beyond pointwise risk assessment to understand how risks emerge and unfold over long-horizon trajectories. In multi-turn interactions, malicious intent can be decomposed across seemingly harmless turns and gradually reconstructed through interaction trajectories, eventually resulting in safety failures. Existing safeguards remain largely reactive, detect-ing manifested violations while lacking the ability to predict latent risk evolution and enable preemptive prevention. To address this limitation, we propose Recast , a safety risk fore-casting framework that advances LLM safeguarding beyond turn-level violation detection to trajectory-level risk predic-tion. Recast first retrieves risk-relevant evidence from both short-term dialogue progression and long-term historical con-text via a dual-scale trajectory view. It then models compo-sitional risk evolution by capturing the current risk configu-ration and its temporal dynamics. Finally, a causal temporal encoder learns latent risk evolution patterns and predicts the distribution of future risk emergence turns. Extensive exper-iments across 7 risk categories show that Recast predicts 

88 .3% of future safety failures with an average lead time of 

2.41 turns, while maintaining a false alarm rate of 12 .3% ,showcasing the effectiveness of trajectory-level forecasting in identifying emerging risks before safety violations occur. 

1 Introduction 

Artificial intelligence is undergoing a revolutionary transi-tion from passive language understanding to autonomous decision-making, largely driven by the emergence of large language models (LLMs) (Achiam et al. 2023; Anthropic 2023; Touvron et al. 2023; Grattafiori et al. 2024; Yang et al. 2025; Guo et al. 2025; Liu et al. 2024a; Glm et al. 2024). Beyond achieving remarkable capabilities in reasoning (Qin et al. 2023; Zhong et al. 2023), programming (Zhang et al. 2023), translation (Peng et al. 2023), and mathematics prob-lem solving (Wei et al. 2022), LLMs are increasingly pow-ering agentic systems capable of autonomously executing complex long-horizon tasks (Yao et al. 2023; Schick et al. 2023; Wu et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2024c). Unlike conven-tional dialogue systems that respond independently to indi-vidual prompts, LLM-based agents must continuously main-tain memory, update task states, and make decisions based

In this context, multi-turn interaction is no longer merely an extension of dialogue, but a fundamental process underlying agentic intelligence. This paradigm transition also reshapes the safety land-scape of agentic systems. Existing efforts have largely fo-cused on single-turn scenarios, where the main goal is to de-termine whether a prompt can directly induce unsafe model behavior (Zou et al. 2023; Wei, Haghtalab, and Steinhardt 2023; Chao et al. 2025; Mehrotra et al. 2024; Liu et al. 2024b; Yu et al. 2024; Lin et al. 2024; Ding et al. 2024). However, in multi-turn interactions, harmful objectives can be strate-gically decomposed into a sequence of locally coherent and seemingly benign requests, each of which appears harmless in isolation but collectively drives the model towards mali-cious outcomes (Russinovich, Salem, and Eldan 2025; Yang et al. 2024; Li et al. 2025; Jiang et al. 2025; Ren et al. 2025; Weng et al. 2025; Rahman et al. 2025; Kong et al. 2025). Therefore, safety risks in agentic systems cannot be viewed as solely pointwise prompt-level violations, but trajectory-level phenomena arising from the temporal accumulation and semantic composition of interactions. Notwithstanding increasing efforts devoted to multi-turn interaction safety, existing approaches remain inherently ret-rospective (Inan et al. 2023; Ghosh et al. 2025; Han et al. 2024; Zeng et al. 2024a; Zhao et al. 2025; Shen et al. 2026; Ma et al. 2026). They usually analyze observed dialogue contexts and leverage historical turns as evidence for as-sessing the safety of the current response. While effective at identifying manifested violations, these methods provide limited understanding of how risks accumulate and trans-arXiv:2607.26820v1 [cs.LG] 29 Jul 2026 form throughout interaction trajectories, making it difficult to anticipate future safety failures before they occur. This lim-itation is particularly critical for multi-turn attacks, where in-dividual turns may appear benign but progressively compose risk-relevant configurations that enable future misuse. This raises an important yet largely unexplored question: given an evolving interaction trajectory, can we forecast whether and when it will transition into an unsafe state in future turns? 

Addressing this challenge requires shifting from retro-spective detection to proactive trajectory-level forecasting. Multi-turn safety risk is neither a simple aggregation of per-turn risks, nor a static property of dialogue history. Instead, it emerges dynamically as new interactions reorganize pre-viously observed evidence and alter its safety relevance. A seemingly benign turn may reinforce existing risk factors and gradually form configurations that precede future violations. Therefore, effective forecasting requires modeling composi-tional risk evolution and anticipating trajectories that may transition towards unsafe states. Motivated by this, we for-mulate multi-turn safety as a compositional risk evolution problem, aiming to characterize how evolving interaction trajectories give rise to future safety violations. To this end, we propose Recast , a trajectory-level safety risk forecasting framework for black-box multi-turn interac-tions. Instead of estimating the risk of the current observed dialogue, Recast forecasts whether and when the interaction trajectory will evolve towards future safety failures. Recast 

consists of three key components: (i) Risk-relevant historical evidence retrieval. Recast captures complementary tempo-ral dependencies by retrieving risk-relevant evidence from both short-term dialogue progression and long-term histori-cal context. (ii) Compositional risk state and transition mod-eling. It constructs a structured risk state from retrieved evi-dence and models its temporal evolution via state transitions, capturing both risk composition and trajectory dynamics. 

(iii) Future risk distribution forecasting. A causal tempo-ral encoder learns latent evolution patterns and predicts the distribution of future risk emergence over subsequent turns. Extensive experiments on 7 risk categories demonstrate the effectiveness of Recast in modeling future risk trajecto-ries, achieving a negative log-likelihood of 0.45 and a mean absolute error of 0.31 for first-risk emergence time forecast-ing. Furthermore, Recast predicts 88 .3% of future safety failures with an average lead time of 2.41 turns while main-taining a false alarm rate of 12 .3% , showing its capability to provide proactive warnings before explicit violations occur. In summary, we make the following key contributions .

• We introduce a new perspective on the safety of multi-turn LLMs by shifting the objective from turn-level violation detection to trajectory-level safety risk forecasting, en-abling early identification of emerging unsafe behaviors. 

• We propose Recast , a safety risk forecasting framework that integrates historical evidence retrieval, structured risk state modeling, and causal trajectory inference to capture the evolution of latent risks during multi-turn interactions. 

• Extensive experiments across 7 risk categories demon-strate that Recast accurately forecasts future risk trajec-tories and provides effective early warning, anticipating 

88 .3% of safety violations with an average lead time of 

2.41 turns while maintaining a 12 .3% false alarm rate. 

2 Problem Formulation 

We consider a black-box multi-turn interaction between a user and a target LLM. At turn i, the user submits a request ui

and receives a response ai. The observed interaction trajec-tory up to turn t is denoted as τ≤t = [( u1, a 1), . . . , (ut, a t)] .After each turn, the safeguard only observes the textual tra-jectory τ≤t, without access to the model parameters, hidden states, logits, system prompts, or internal safety mechanisms. Unlike conventional safeguards that examine whether the current response violates safety policies, we study trajectory-level risk forecasting, aiming to predict whether and when the ongoing interaction will evolve into a future safety risk. Formally, let t⋆ denote the first turn at which the inter-action trajectory exhibits a predefined safety risk. At any pre-risk turn t < t⋆, the remaining time to risk emer-gence is defined as dt = t⋆ − t. Given a forecasting hori-zon H, our goal is to learn a predictor F (·) that esti-mates the conditional distribution of future risk emergence: 

F (τ≤t) → πt, where πt = [ πt(1) , . . . , π t(H), π t(> H )] is a discrete time-to-risk distribution. For each h ∈ { 1, . . . , H },

πt(h) = P (dt = h | τ≤t) represents the probability that the safety risk emerges exactly h turns after the current turn, whereas πt(> H ) = P (dt > H | τ≤t) represents the prob-ability that no safety risk emerges within the next H turns. Therefore, πt characterizes both the likelihood of future risk emergence and its expected occurrence time. 

3 Method 

Method overview . Motivated by the observation that multi-turn safety risks emerge through evolving interaction tra-jectories, we propose Recast , a trajectory-level safety risk forecasting framework. Unlike existing safeguards that de-tect manifested risks in observed dialogues, Recast predicts how current interactions may evolve towards future safety violations. Our key insight is to model multi-turn safety as compositional risk evolution : seemingly benign dialogue fragments can be reactivated and recomposed by subsequent turns into risk-prone configurations. These configurations form the current risk state, while state transitions character-ize their temporal evolution and provide signals for future risk forecasting. As illustrated in Figure 2, Recast consists of three components: (1) risk-relevant historical evidence re-trieval , which extracts evidence from short-term progression and long-term context; (2) compositional risk state and tran-sition modeling , which constructs risk states and captures their evolution across turns; and (3) future risk distribution forecasting , which employs a causal temporal encoder to predict the distribution of future risk emergence times.

Future Risk Distribution Forecasting 

Intuitively, forecasting future risk requires modeling not only the current turn state but also the trajectory through which it emerges. With the temporal risk states and transitions, 

Recast employs a causal temporal encoder to learn latent evolution patterns and predict the distribution of future risk emergence across multiple horizons. 

Causal risk evolution encoding . We arrange the risk states and transitions at every turn into a temporal order 

[( s1, r1), . . . , (st, rt)] . This sequence characterizes both the risk configuration at each turn and the dynamics leading to the current state. We then apply a causal temporal encoder:

4 Experiment 

We conduct extensive experiments to evaluate the effective-ness of Recast , seeking to answer the following questions: 

• RQ1: Can Recast accurately forecast safety risks and re-duce the success rate of multi-turn jailbreak attacks? How about the additional latency introduced by Recast ?

• RQ2: How effective is Recast under diverse forecasting settings and interaction conditions? 

• RQ3: How does each component of Recast contribute to trajectory-level safety risk forecasting? 

• RQ4: Does Recast capture interpretable risk evolution patterns before explicit safety violations emerge? 

Experimental Setup 

Training dataset . To model multi-turn risk evolution, we construct a safety trajectory dataset through a four-stage pipeline. First, we collect multi-turn attack trajectories from public resources and normalize them to balance risk cat-egories and attack patterns. Second, we perform controlled length augmentation and align trajectory-length distributions to mitigate length bias. Third, we generate hard benign sam-ples by transforming risk-inducing turns into safety-oriented contexts, enabling the model to distinguish genuine risk evo-lution from benign analysis of sensitive topics. Finally, we unify all samples into a standardized format and split the dataset to avoid evaluation leakage. The resulting dataset contains 10,000 trajectories, including 5,000 harmful and 5,000 benign samples across 7 risk categories. and trigger warnings when Rt > τ a. Early warning rate (EWR), False alarm rate (FAR), and Mean lead turn (MLT) measure warning coverage, false alarms, and lead time, re-spectively. (iii) Online defense. We evaluate practical effec-tiveness by measuring ∆ASR , i.e., the reduction in attack success rate compared with the undefended setting. 

Attack baselines . We evaluate Recast against 4 multi-turn attack methods: ActorAttack (Ren et al. 2025) and Red Queen (Jiang et al. 2025), which are included in training and constitute seen attack families; and ICON (Lin et al. 2026) and X-Teaming (Rahman et al. 2025), which are strictly ex-cluded from training and serve as unseen attack families for evaluating cross-attack generalization. 

Safeguard baselines . We compare 5 safeguard baselines from 3 categories: Guardrail model , including Llama-Guard-3-8B (Inan et al. 2023) and Qwen3Guard-Gen-8B (Zhao et al. 2025); History-aware safeguards , including THRD (Ma et al. 2026); and Prompt-based defenses , including Intent Analy-sis (Zhang et al. 2025) and LLM-as-Judge (Perez et al. 2022). 

Target models . We evaluate all attacks on 3 representative LLMs, including GPT-4o-2024-11-20, Llama-3-8B-Instruct, and Qwen2-7B-Instruct. To ensure deterministic results, we set both temperature and top_p to 0.

Representative case . We then investigate a representative case involving vehicle theft. As shown in Figure 7, both harm-ful and benign trajectories initially discuss vehicle theft pre-vention techniques over the first 4 turns, and Recast assigns low risk scores to both trajectories ( ≤ 0.11 ). At turn 5, the harmful trajectory begins to compose previously discussed information into a vulnerability-oriented context, causing the risk score to increase sharply to 0.85 and exceed the warning threshold τa = 0 .5, while the prevention-oriented trajectory remains at a low risk level. 5 Related Work 

Safety risks in multi-turn interactions . Recent studies re-veal that safety risks in LLMs can emerge from the cumula-tive evolution of multi-turn interactions. Crescendo (Russi-novich, Salem, and Eldan 2025) gradually escalates benign conversations toward harmful objectives; Red Queen (Jiang et al. 2025) hides malicious intent in harm-prevention con-texts; ActorAttack (Ren et al. 2025) exploits semantically related interactions; and FITD (Weng et al. 2025) decom-poses unsafe goals into progressive requests. More adaptive attacks, such as X-Teaming (Rahman et al. 2025) and Tem-pest (Zhou and Arel 2025), further leverage agent collabora-tion or search-based optimization to refine attack trajectories. However, existing studies focus on improving attack effec-tiveness and provide limited insight into how safety risks evolve throughout interactions. In contrast, Recast inves-tigates risk evolution at the trajectory level and forecasts emerging safety risks before explicit violations occur. 

Defenses against multi-turn attacks . Existing safeguards for multi-turn risks can be categorized into three groups. 

Guardrail models , including Llama Guard (Inan et al. 2023), Qwen3Guard (Zhao et al. 2025), WildGuard (Han et al. 2024), and ShieldGemma (Zeng et al. 2024a), classify the safety of observed inputs or outputs. Prompt-based defenses 

leverage LLM reasoning to assess intent and safety, includ-ing Intent Analysis (Zhang et al. 2025), LLM-as-Judge (Perez et al. 2022), and AutoDefense (Zeng et al. 2024b). History-aware safeguards , such as THRD (Ma et al. 2026) and Turn-Gate (Shen et al. 2026), incorporate dialogue history to im-prove risk assessment. Nevertheless, these approaches are largely retrospective, detecting risks manifested in observed interactions rather than forecasting future risk evolution. Unlike previous safeguards, Recast formulates multi-turn safety as a trajectory-level forecasting problem, predicting the likelihood of future safety risks before violations emerge. 

6 Conclusion 

We study trajectory-level safety risk forecasting for black-box multi-turn interactions, shifting the focus of multi-turn safe-guards from retrospective detection to proactive risk antici-pation. We propose Recast , which retrieves risk-relevant ev-idence from short-term dialogue progression and long-term historical context. It then constructs structured risk states and models their temporal evolution to forecast the distribution of future risk emergence. Extensive experiments demonstrate that Recast accurately predicts risk evolution and provides effective early warnings before explicit safety violations oc-cur. Our work highlights the potential of trajectory-level mod-eling for building proactive multi-turn safeguards. References

Construction of Training Dataset 

In multi-turn interactions, harmful objectives are often not explicitly revealed in a single request but gradually emerge through the accumulation and composition of seemingly be-nign turns. Therefore, our data construction aims to cap-ture how distributed risk signals evolve into safety failures, enabling trajectory-level risk forecasting. The construction follows three principles: 

• Broad attack coverage. The dataset should encompass diverse risk categories and attack strategies, allowing the model to learn generalizable risk evolution patterns rather than specific attack templates. 

• Temporal progression signals. The dataset should pre-serve how risks develop across turns, with each harmful trajectory annotated by its first-failure turn to provide su-pervision for forecasting. • Challenging benign counterparts. The dataset should in-clude hard benign trajectories that share similar topics, terminology, and progression patterns with harmful ones but remain safe, encouraging the model to distinguish risk evolution from superficial similarities. Based on these principles, we construct the training corpus through a four-stage pipeline. 

Stage 1: Diverse Source Curation and Regularization .We collect multi-turn attack trajectories from three public sources: CKA-Agent, Red Queen, and ActorAttack. Despite their different attack mechanisms, these methods decompose harmful objectives into sequences of plausible subgoals. To improve diversity and reduce distribution bias, we map all samples to a unified risk taxonomy and perform balanced sampling across risk categories and attack strategies. 

Stage 2: Trajectory Length Expansion . Short trajectories provide limited evidence of risk evolution, while length im-balance may introduce spurious correlations between dia-logue length and safety labels. We thus expand trajectories to increase their average length and balance the length distribu-tion. Harmful trajectories are extended by adding contextual setup and intermediate subgoals while preserving their orig-inal intent. Corresponding benign trajectories are expanded with comparable length and progression patterns but without leading to safety failures, reducing length-based bias. 

Stage 3: Hard Benign Trajectory Construction . To pre-vent the model from relying on sensitive topics or key-words, hard benign trajectories are constructed that pre-serve the context of harmful counterparts while transforming failure-supporting structures into defence-oriented reason-ing. Specifically, an auxiliary LLM first identifies turns that support failure, another LLM then rewrites these towards safe objectives and a safety evaluator finally verifies that the resulting trajectory remains harmless. While the resulting pairs share similar surface characteristics, they differ in their organisation of information: harmful trajectories accumulate evidence towards unsafe outcomes, whereas benign trajecto-ries redirect the same context towards safe resolutions. 

Stage 4: Unified Annotation and Standardization . Finally, we consolidate all constructed samples into a unified corpus and convert them into a standardized format. This includes trajectory content, risk category, attack strategy, and failure annotations. To verify annotation reliability, we additionally conduct human verification on a random subset of 1,000 responses, obtaining an agreement rate over 86% with the original annotations. All identified disagreements are subse-quently adjudicated and corrected. 

Additional Experimental Results 

Cross-attack generalization . We also investigate whether 

Recast transfers beyond the attack structures observed dur-ing training. Tables 5 and 6 separately report the results on seen attack families (ActorAttack and Red Queen) and un-seen families (ICON and X-Teaming). Specifically, Recast 

reduces the average ASR to 55 .3% on unseen attacks, cor-responding to a 31 .6% relative reduction, while reducing it to 36 .6% on seen attacks with a 41 .2% reduction. When combined with Qwen3Guard, the ASR is further reduced to 48 .5% and 32 .3% on unseen and seen attacks, yielding relative reductions of 39 .9% and 48 .2% , respectively. These results demonstrate that the learned trajectory-level risk sig-nals transfer to attack families that are absent from training, while the remaining seen-unseen gap reflects the additional challenge introduced by cross-attack distribution shifts.

Additional Analysis of Case Study 

We analyze a case from the Financial Crime category con-cerning vehicle anti-theft mechanisms. The harmful trajec-tory seeks actionable assistance for bypassing these defence mechanisms, whereas the benign counterpart discusses the same technical factors for auditing and prevention. Table 8 summarizes the key semantics of the dialogue. 

How Prior Context Supports Warning . Figure 9(a) exam-ines the effect of context on warnings. Removing the broad trend context in T1 reduces the risk score of T5 to 0.724 ,whereas removing T2, T3, or T4 lowers it to 0.125 , 0.004 , and 

0.228 , respectively. The particularly decrease after removing T3 indicates that linking target vehicles to theft methods is critical when T5 reframes the discussion around defensive gaps. Therefore, the warning depends not on T5 alone, but on how it connects evidence from earlier turns. 

How Forecast Evolves Toward Failure . Figure 9(b) shows how the forecast evolves towards failure. After T4 lists mul-tiple anti-theft technologies, the harmful branch still assigns 

0.898 probability to d > 3, indicating that sensitive technical content alone does not trigger a warning. After T5 links these defences through their integration gaps, this probability falls to 0.150 , while the mass shifts to d = 2 (0.413 ) and d = 3 

(0.420 ), yielding a within-horizon risk of 0.850 . At T6, when the user asks how to defeat the disconnected layers system-atically, the risk rises to 0.999 , one turn before the annotated T7 failure. In contrast, the benign branch also discusses the same topic but refuses exploit-oriented requests and keeps T5 focused on prevention, retaining 0.995 probability on d > 3.The forecast therefore captures how information develops across turns rather than focusing on the topic alone. Figure 9(c) shows that this change is not due to the passage of time. Compared with the T4 distribution shifted forward by one bucket, the harmful forecast after T5 changes substan-tially (JSD = 0 .362 ), whereas the benign forecast remains almost unchanged (JSD < 0.001 ). Thus, the shift towards near-term failure is driven by the new relationship introduced at T5 and not by a simple reduction in remaining distance.
