Abstract —Over the past decade, decision trees have been used to represent controllers (a.k.a. policies) in an explainable way, with dtControl 2 as a current state-of-the-art tool. However, for systems that are large or have many corner cases, even such representations tend to be too complex and not human-comprehensible. Unfortunately, reducing the size of the decision tree is not straightforward, as missing just a single crucial case might result in an incorrect controller. We tackle this issue in the setting of Markov decision processes, extending 

dtControl 2 by “ ε” functionality: Given an allowed imprecision 

ε ≥ 0, we construct a smaller decision tree, distilling the essence of the controller, while still guaranteeing its ε-optimality. This enables us to provide tunably simpler explanations, omitting a controllable amount of detail. Our tool constructs decision trees that are orders of magnitude smaller than the state of the art. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Need for Compact and Explainable Controllers. Controller synthesis [64, 61] automatically constructs a controller for a non-deterministic system so that it satisfies a given specifica-tion. While being a tempting alternative to error-prone human-designed controllers, several hurdles prevent widespread adop-tion: Automatically synthesised controllers are typically rep-resented explicitly as unstructured, large tables , assigning to each state of the system an action to take. In contrast, numerous practical purposes require controllers to be concise 

and explainable . Regarding size, it may be challenging to implement tables with millions of rows, especially on embed-ded devices with limited hardware. Regarding explainability, such a table’s sheer size and lack of structure prohibits even domain experts from understanding the controller. In addition to engineers’ natural reluctance to use controllers they cannot understand (and thus sensibly maintain and certify), explaining the controller contributes significantly to validating the whole modelling process. Finally, explainability per se may even become a legal requirement [39, 59] whenever AI techniques are aiding the process. A plethora of surveys on explainability underline its relevance [32, 6, 5, 68, 31, 55, 56]. 

Decision trees (DT) are among the most explainable for-malisms, being both transparent and interpretable, e.g. [5, 60, 23]. For a decade, they have been used for representing controllers, e.g. [52, 14, 9, 8, 69, 71, 28] and as such found various usages, namely for detecting bugs [14, 8], as part of modelling [45], or for explaining complex strategies [16]. Mature tool support is available [8, 4]. However, in an in-teresting contrast to (generally imprecise) DT learning, the safety-criticality motivated these tools to focus on precise representation of controllers, i.e. for every state providing an optimal (winning/safe) action. 

Our focus is on representing controllers arising in Markov decision processes (MDPs) [63]. There, representing the full controller completely is often not necessary, as many states are only reached with small probability and behaving sub-optimally in them does not significantly change the outcome, cf. [48, 53]. Moreover, most scalable methods for controller synthesis do not provide an optimal controller, but only an ε-optimal one [17, 33], i.e. whose performance is ε-close to op-timal for a given ε ≥ 0. Exploiting this fact, we allow the DT to ignore some decisions, provided the resulting performance is still ε-optimal. DTs [60, 65], though comprehensibility also depends on predicate complexity, not size alone [37]. A related idea was presented in [14] for reachability objectives, where simulation was used to heuristically learn only the decision in frequently visited states, ignoring others. However, this heuristic was “aggressive”, potentially compromising ε-optimality. Moreover, the prototypical implementation of [14] is unavailable. In contrast, we provide a self-contained, richly parametrisable tool that guarantees ε-optimality of the result-ing controller across the full standard spectrum of objectives. 

Our contribution is the tool dtControl 2+ ε: It ex-tends dtControl 2 [8], most importantly by adding the functionality—dubbed “ ε”—to construct smaller and more explainable DTs , exploiting the allowed imprecision ε.The workflow description (Fig. 2, Section III-C) outlines all the technical novelties. The key enabler is our tight coupling of the tool with the probabilistic model checker 

Storm [36], based on which we provide numerous ways for DT size reduction while guaranteeing ε-optimality: (i) The initial controller is permissive, containing multiple optimal

Notably, our tool is the only one supporting linear temporal logic (LTL) properties, see Appendix A. 

Improvements over other tools. Our evaluation compares to 

dtControl 2 [8], dtPaynt [4], and dtNESt [3]. Already for precise representation ( ε = 0 ), dtControl 2+ ε achieves order-of-magnitude improvements over dtControl 2 due to the improved initial controller, safe heuristics, and automatic predicate suggestions. dtPaynt mostly times out, and on over half the remaining benchmarks produces larger DTs. 

dtNESt is the strongest competitor; still, dtControl 2+ ε

produces DTs at most half the size in 23 of 38 bench-marks and an order of magnitude smaller in 10. Moreover, 

dtControl 2+ ε is best at exploiting allowed imprecision: when allowing ε = 10 −2, we obtain single-node DTs for nearly half the benchmarks, exposing that focusing on asingle action is ε-optimal — a previously unobserved fact. In contrast, dtNESt mostly fails due to internal errors and, surprisingly, may even produce larger DTs when ε > 0.

Related work. dtControl 2 [8], the direct predecessor of 

dtControl 2+ ε, comes from a stream of work on precise 

controller representation [14, 9, 7, 8, 43]. The closest work to our ε-approach is [14]: it prunes the DT and weights learning by visit frequency from simulation. Both ideas are aggressive , i.e., they can compromise ε-optimality, and [14] handles this by restarting from scratch if the result is unsound. In contrast, dtControl 2+ ε cleanly separates safe from aggressive heuristics, guaranteeing ε-optimality with-out restarting, and builds on dtControl 2 ’s tailored DT algorithms rather than a general-purpose ML package [14, Sec. 6.1]. Verification of the resulting DT is automated in 

dtControl 2+ ε. In contrast, [14] required manual binary search over hyperparameters. Finally, [14] covers only reacha-bility and uses a state ×action ( S×A → { 0, 1}) representation, while dtControl 2+ ε supports all standard objectives and the S → A representation of the dtControl 2 tradition. 

dtPaynt [4] finds policies representable as minimal-depth DTs via abstraction-refinement and SMT. However, its scal-ability is limited, timing out on most of our benchmarks. 

dtNESt [3] builds on both dtControl 2 and dtPaynt : it constructs a DT with dtControl 2 and iteratively shrinks subtrees with dtPaynt , feeding the result back to both tools for local search. This is orthogonal to our approach and can be seen as an elaborate pruning step; a tight cou-pling of dtControl 2+ ε with dtNESt could combine both strengths. OMDT [71] finds optimally performing trees of a prescribed size, but provides no error control and does not scale beyond depth 3; it is significantly outperformed by 

dtPaynt [4, Sec. 5]. Standard DT learning [52] can represent controllers but is not tailored to the task of finding ε-optimal controllers that can be represented by small DTs. Furthermore, representing RL policies as DTs has received considerable attention, e.g. via direct RL frameworks [69, 12], policy gradients [25], distillation [46], and dynamic program-ming [26]. DTs have also been used for shields [15]. The RL community has also studied approximating value functions via linear programming [27], domain-specific languages [70, 13], and shallow neural networks [42, 66]. We do not compare to these, as they do not provide any control of the (im)precision. Binary decision diagrams (BDDs) are an alternative to DTs but are hindered in this context by bit-blasting of non-Boolean variables, and fixed variable ordering resulting in larger representations [7, 8]. Recent work [19] partially closes this gap. Neural networks are fundamentally less explainable than DTs [5]. II. T ECHNICAL PRELIMINARIES

B. Decision Trees 

A decision tree (DT) T [57] is a binary tree where leafs are labelled by actions and inner nodes are labelled by predicates 

ρ ∈ Preds , ρ : S → { true , false }. States typically comprise a tuple of variables, e.g. x- and y-coordinates, allowing for compact symbolic predicates, e.g. x ≤ 2, see Fig. 1 (right). A DT yields an action for a given state s as follows: Start from the root node. In an inner node, evaluate its predicate ρ(s)

and go to the left or right child if it evaluates to true or false ,respectively. In a leaf node labelled a, pick a if a ∈ A(s), and otherwise uniformly at random choose an available action. We denote the last case by the special label Unif . We write T (s)

for the action (or uniform choice) yielded by the DT T in state s and identify the DT with the corresponding controller. 

Remark 1: Note that the above subtly assumes that the system can at runtime “pick any action (at random)”. Since even “do nothing” is an action to be modelled, we believe this assumption is natural. However, if this is not the case, one can simply disable related heuristics ( EqVal and AllOpt ,defined below); the rest of our approach does not rely on them. Constructing a minimal DT is NP-complete [38], so we base our approach on the standard heuristic-based algorithm outlined in Algorithm 1. Here, the input dataset is a controller, mapping states to actions. If all given states use the same action, the algorithm constructs a leaf node with that common action. Otherwise, we attempt to split the dataset using a pred-icate ρ∗, chosen from a set Preds using an impurity measure 

Impurity , which intuitively assesses how “heterogeneous” 

ρ(D) and ¬ρ(D) are (where ρ(D) = {(s, a ) ∈ D | ρ(s)} and 

¬ρ(D) analogously). This heuristically indicates how many further splits are required to represent the dataset. Thus, we insert the predicate minimizing impurity as a tree node, split the dataset accordingly and recurse on the two smaller dataset which only contain choices for a subset S′ ⊆ S.

Remark 2: We point out two differences to the definition in [8]: Firstly, we label leaves with a single action, whereas they use sets of actions. While our theory is also applicable to using sets, there are exponentially many sets of actions, and determinising , i.e. committing to one safe choice, reduces the final DT’s size and increases readability. (We, however, do employ non-determinism in intermediate steps, see below.) Secondly, [8] ensures that π(s) ∈ A(s) for every s, i.e. the DT only recommends available actions. This section sketches the main ideas and overall structure of dtControl 2+ ε. The tool aims to efficiently create small, explainable decision tree controllers for probabilistic systems. The key novelty and difference to dtControl 2 is employing the information about the model and the allowed imprecision ε.Our goal then can be stated as follows: 

PROBLEM STATEMENT 

Given an MDP, objective, and precision ε ≥ 0, obtain a (small) decision tree T such that |VT (¯ s) − V(¯ s)| ≤ ε.(Note that ε = 0 means that T should represent an opti-mal controller.) The key challenge is to exploit the allowed imprecision while maintaining all: scalability (in contrast to, e.g., [3]), correctness (in contrast to, e.g., [14], which relies on discovering all important states during simulation, which sometimes yields a DT that is not epsilon-optimal), and completeness (if a decision tree exists, our tool will always find one by omitting aggressive heuristics when necessary). 

A. Illustrative Example 

We illustrate the core ideas of our tool: Consider a robot 

in a grid world environment on a hillside. The robot can move in the cardinal directions →, ←, ↑, ↓, but always has a 10% 

chance of sliding south instead. The goal is to reach the repair location { while avoiding the obstacles þ. Fig. 1 illustrates the environment and a near-optimal controller ( ε = 0 .001 )obtained by our tool. Modelling this scenario as an MDP, we can use a probabilis-tic model checker (like Storm [36]) to compute a controller achieving the optimal reachability probability. Despite the simplicity of the example, this optimal controller consists of dozens of state-action mappings. The DT computed by 

dtControl 2 , accurately representing this controller, uses 33 nodes (see Fig. 7 (left) in Appendix B). In contrast, 

dtControl 2+ ε uses information about the model to apply several reductions, e.g. that states with x = 0 are not reached under the optimal controller and there is no need to learn choices for them. This and similar observations already allow to reduce the DT size to 21 nodes (see Fig. 7 (right)). This reduced DT still achieves the optimal value. By allowing for an imprecision ε, we can afford to make sub-optimal choices in some states and prune parts of the DT that are only relevant with very small probability. Concretely, allowing a minuscule deviation of ε = 0 .001 , we can reduce the DT to five nodes and only three decision paths (see Fig. 1 (right)). This DT captures the “essence” of the behaviour: Go up to the top border, then go right to the border, then go down. Note that this controller is indeed not optimal, as it risks repeatedly sliding down into an obstacle by going up rather then moving to the right.

C. Tool Structure 

We summarise the major steps of the workflow of 

dtControl 2+ ε (see Fig. 2). Technical insights are provided in the following section. 

Step 1: Model Building and Solving. We use stormpy to construct the provided model (supporting both PRISM and JANI format), parse the objective, and compute the optimal value and an optimal controller for the given objective. 

Step 2: Dataset Construction. Using the optimal value and controller, we construct an initial dataset D, where any controller consistent with it (i.e., T (s) ∈ D(s) for all s

where D is defined) is guaranteed to be ( ε-)optimal; espe-cially, these can be permissive (non-deterministic), enabling determinisation heuristics. Moreover, we identify and remove 

irrelevant states, i.e. where the controller’s choice does not affect the obtained value, using model- and objective-specific information. In contrast, for dtControl 2 , the user has to manually obtain a (non-permissive) controller from Storm 

for which dtControl 2 then learns all decisions, including for irrelevant states. We discuss the details in Section IV. 

Step 3: DT Learning. We employ our adaptation of 

dtControl 2 to learn a DT based on the reduced dataset. Among other improvements, we extract model-specific pred-icates as potential decision nodes in the tree and employ specialised impurity calculations and pruning techniques. We provide details in Section V.

Following the procedure of our tool, we first provide in-sights into the creation of the initial dataset, which is then reduced using our heuristics. 

A. Creation of the Initial Dataset/Controller 

When creating the initial dataset, we can either focus on a controller that is itself small and only contains one action per state, or we can try to start with a permissive controller. For DT learning, a permissive dataset, i.e. one that maps states to a set of allowed actions, is often advantageous [7, 8]. When learning a DT consistent with a permissive dataset ( T (s) ∈D(s) for all s where D is defined), it is often possible to terminate the DT construction earlier by using that actions are shared between states and determinizing by choosing one of them. For example, consider the optimal controller π for the MDP in Fig. 3 with π(s) = l and π(t) = r. DT construction would yield a 3-node tree when representing the controller exactly. By additionally allowing action r in s, we can obtain a simpler 1-node tree that always picks r. Previously, the initial dataset was not permissive because tools like Storm export only deterministic optimal controllers. While, as discussed, a more permissive dataset generally yields good results, it has the disadvantage that more states are potentially reachable and need to be considered while learning. In particular, if Unif ∈D(s) then all potential successors of s need to be considered. Additionally, adding actions to the permissive dataset requires thought to not break optimality. 

Example 1: One might be tempted to simply allow all “optimal” actions, i.e. those yielding optimal value, by D(s) = 

{a ∈ A(s) | V(s, a ) = V(s)}. Indeed, this is correct for, e.g., safety. However, for other objectives such as reachability and total reward, it is not: Observe that all states and actions in Fig. 3 have a value of 1. If both actions are allowed for s and t,then the controller picking r in s and l in t is dataset-consistent but never reaches the target, yielding value 0. Intuitively, this is because it makes no “progress”. Moreover, there is no unique inclusion-maximal permissive dataset; both {s → { l}, t →{l, r }} and {s → { l, r }, t → { r}} are optimal. Thus, we introduce two methods for obtaining some optimal permissive dataset. 

GoodActions : Building on [20], we allow all actions with optimal value that reduce the distance to the goal with non-zero probability. In the above example, this yields D = {s →{l}, t → { r}} . While this cannot break symmetry when states have the same distance to the goal, it often adds actions. For objectives not requiring progress, e.g. safety, GoodActions 

adds all actions with optimal value.

Based on the heuristics named above, we propose three variants of obtaining an initial dataset, or, in other words, the initial controller. 

Controller Firstly, we directly use the dataset given by the optimal controller, i.e. D(s) = {π(a)}, without any permissive choices. 

Permissive Secondly, as a fully permissive approach, we use the dataset computed by GoodActions and EqVal ,giving the best chances for determinization, but poten-tially increasing the size of the reachable state space. 

Combined Lastly, we restrict the Permissive dataset to the set of states SR reached by the optimal controller from the initial states by removing all actions leaving SR, i.e. those with δ(s, a )( s′) > 0 for some s′ /∈ SR.

Remark 5: Obtaining a dataset this way is fundamentally different from obtaining an ε-optimal controller and using that for DT-learning. Such a controller would assign one specific action to every single state, even if that choice is entirely inconsequential, leaving us with a much more re-stricted dataset. Moreover, we lose all “imprecision budget” for simplification – as the starting controller may already be sub-optimal, any additional imprecision might compromise the overall guarantees. 

B. Dataset Reduction 

After the computation of the initial dataset, we can identify states that can be removed entirely from the dataset, as choosing any actions in these states ensures optimality. We list the methods we use to identify these states. 

AllOpt : If a dataset allows all actions in a state s, i.e. 

D(s) = A(s), then it can be removed. Recall that if the DT suggests an action not available in s, then one of the available actions is chosen uniformly at random. This in particular removes states with a single available action, i.e. |A(s)| = 1 .

V. D ETAILS OF STEP 3: DT L EARNING 

Our tool provides three novelties regarding safe DT learn-ing: Firstly, we automatically extract relevant predicates from the high-level description of the model in PRISM language. Secondly, we improve the impurity calculation using a model-specific measure of importance. Thirdly, we apply aggressive early stopping and pruning heuristics, which was previously impossible to do safely, as ε-optimality could not be guaran-teed without re-validation. 

A. Preds 

We offer axis-aligned and linear splits as already imple-mented in dtControl 2 . This split divides the input space by choosing one variable and comparing it to a constant. An example is y ≤ 0 from the example in Fig. 1. Additionally, we propose a new set of predicates: When the model is given in the PRISM modelling language [51], we can access the expressions defining its dynamics. Using the transition guards as predicates gives us a fully automated way of exploiting domain knowledge and obtaining predicates that are likely relevant for the behaviour of the MDP. This improves on the semi-automatic approach in [8], where templates for predicates were manually provided using domain knowledge. 

B. Impurity 

As previously mentioned, the detailed analysis in [8] suggested that the best choice for Impurity measure is Shannon’s entropy [67], which is defined as H(D) =

P(ρ(D)) log P(ρ(D)) + P(¬ρ(D)) log P(¬ρ(D)) . Usually, all states are weighed uniformly when computing the probability; we denote impurity with uniform weights by Imp_Uniform .We complement the idea by further weighting the impact of states with a model-specific measure of importance. We provide four heuristics: 

Imp_CAV15 and Imp_Simulation : Imp_CAV15 de-notes the idea of [14]: simulate the system under the op-timal controller several times and weigh each state with the number of times it is sampled. As states may get weight 0, this importance heuristic implicitly reduces the dataset, potentially compromising ε-optimality. Thus, our vari-ant Imp_Simulation assigns a positive weight of 10 −6 to unsampled states to ensure ε-optimality. 

Imp_Agency : We use the values computed by the reduc-tion method Agency (maximum vs. minimum probability to reach the goal) as weights. Removing states satisfying 

Agency is equivalent to assigning weight 0 in the impu-rity computation, so this importance heuristic generalises the 

Agency reduction. For other objectives, the weights are accordingly adjusted, e.g., the difference in maximum and minimum reward. 

Imp_CoE (Cost of Error): While Agency captures the error incurred if we would choose the worst action in all states, 

Imp_CoE considers the impact of only selecting the worst action in one state. A precise computation can in the worst case require a model checking query for every state (since states can depend on themselves). Thus, we approximate this by comparing the value of the worst and the best action of a state. Observe that this heuristic generalises AllOpt , as the cost of error in states with equal value on all actions is 0. To ensure ε-optimality, similar to Imp_Simulation , we add a small positive weight to all states. 

C. Early-Stopping 

The idea of stopping early is standard in machine learn-ing, but has not been employed in the context of controller representation, since so far, optimality had to be preserved. Exploiting the allowed imprecisions, we adopt this idea as follows. When the current impurity is below a given threshold 

θ ≥ 0, we return a leaf node. This is an extension of the stopping condition of dtControl 2 (Line 1 of Algorithm 1) which requires all states to agree on an action. As the choice of label is no longer straightforward, we label the leaf with an action determined through weighted voting, described below. We start with a threshold of θ = 0 .5 and decrease it by 0.1 if the resulting controller is not ε-optimal; in the worst case (when the heuristic always increases the imprecision to more than ε), this results in a precise DT after 6 iterations. 

Weighted Voting: Our Early-Stopping and Pruning heuris-tics require to decide on an action for a node when not all states agree. We employ the following weighted voting approach: Each state s has weight w(s) according to the 

Impurity method we used for building the DT (see Sec-tion V-B). Then, denoting by D the given dataset and by X

the set of states in the training dataset that reach the node, we select arg max a∈A

P

> s∈X,a ∈D (s)

w(s), i.e. the action with the highest weight when summing up the weights of states for which this action is allowed by the dataset/controller. 

D. Pruning 

As part of building a reduced DT, we also apply a greedy pruning approach (see Algorithm 2), meaning that we try to delete nodes that have little influence on the value of the controller. In each iteration, we go over all nodes which have leaves as both their children. For each of these nodes, we obtain a new DT by replacing that inner node with a leaf whose label is determined by weighted voting as for Early-Stopping. Then we model-check each of these candidates. If any of these DTs still satisfy the precision constraints, we choose the one that decreases the value the least and repeat the process with the new DT. As this requires several model checking queries, the process is quite expensive. Nonetheless, since our goal is to obtain DTs that are as small as possible, it can be worth investing the time. Note that even when early stopping is enabled, pruning can still reduce the size of the tree. Early stopping focuses on the quality of the prediction with respect to the given dataset, while pruning focuses on the learnt controller. This means that some splits, while necessary to represent the dataset well, are in the end not really important to the controller. We comment on two further related mechanisms: Firstly, in [9], a heuristic called “safe pruning” was presented. However, this heuristic was exploiting the non-determinism present in the given controller, and could only remove nodes if the resulting controller was still exactly represented. In contrast, we allow for the resulting controller to be less defined, potentially worsening its performance; then, we rely on the model checking to ensure ε-optimality. Secondly, the core idea of [3] is to take the DT constructed by dtControl 2 and then prune it by looking for subtrees that can be reduced using the exhaustive search of [2]. In principle, this post-processing can also be applied to our output; however, the prototypical implementation of [3] does not allow for this. VI. E VALUATION

A. Experimental Setup 

Configurations. dtControl 2+ ε has 12 different con-figurations, resulting from three initial datasets Con-troller , Permissive , and Combined , and four impurity measures Imp_Simulation , Imp_Agency , Imp_CoE ,

Imp_Uniform . We consider three variants of these configu-rations: our best , i.e. running all 12 configurations and picking the smallest DT; a portfolio of three configurations to im-prove practical scalability ( Controller +Imp_Simulation ,

Permissive +Imp_CoE , and Combined +Imp_Agency ); and 

Combined +Imp_Agency as example single configuration. 

Competitors are dtControl 2 [8], dtPaynt [4], 

dtNESt [3], and “CAV15”, our reimplementation of [14] (the original code is unavailable). Appendix E1 provides the exact versions and describes technical challenges. 

Benchmarks. We select models from two sources: first, all instances from the quantitative verification benchmark set [35] which are in PRISM language and have between 40 000 and 

500 000 states; for models with more than one supported property, we take the first of each type, e.g., only one for reachability and one for total reward. Second, all instances used in [8] which are in PRISM language, have at least 100 

states, and are not included (with some parametrisation) in our first selection. Overall, we obtain 38 benchmarks: 34 from the first selection and 4 from the second. While dtControl 2+ ε

supports other modelling languages, we focus on PRISM 

models, as these allow automatic extraction of predicates. For LTL, there are unfortunately neither any established non-trivial (i.e. beyond a simple Until) MDP benchmarks [1, Sec. 5], nor a competitor able to handle them (see Appendix A). 

Quality Measure and Plots. We collect the runtime of the tools and the size (i.e. its number of nodes) of constructed DTs. We present our data as quantile plots, which are suitable for comparing several methods at once. A point (x, y ) for method X indicates that the x best instances of X measured at most 

y in the considered metric (size or time). 

Execution Environment. All executions for our benchmarks were run on a server with AMD EPYC 9274F CPUs inside a Docker container restricted to a single core and 16 GB of RAM. The timeout for dtControl 2+ ε is 5 minutes for model solving, 10 minutes for dataset creation and DT learn-ing, and an additional 5 minutes for pruning (if applicable). The competitors have a timeout of 20 minutes.

Pnueli-Zuck randomized mutual exclusion ( n=5 ) [35, 62] 

models n processes competing for a shared resource. The non-determinism arises from the scheduler choosing which process to activate and from internal branching within each process. The property maximises the probability that process 1 eventually reaches state p1 = 10 on its way to the critical section. The DT states, that, whenever p1 = 1, it should be updated to p1 = 2. This sends it along a path that reaches p1 = 10 with probability 1. Consequently, the optimal value is 1 in every reachable state, making all scheduling and branching choices simultaneously optimal. dtControl 2 is unaware of this and encodes a decision for every state, yielding 

171 373 nodes. dtControl 2+ ε reduces this to a single-node 

DT, showing that following a simple rule satisfies the property. 

CSMA time minimisation ( N =2 , K=6 ) [35] models N =2 

stations competing for a shared bus with exponential backoff up to K=6 collision rounds. Each station checks whether the bus is free, and if so, sends its message. Otherwise, it waits for a random amount of time. The property time _min minimises the expected time until stations have delivered their messages. 

dtControl 2 yields a 125 -node DT and dtNESt a 25 -node DT, while dtControl 2+ ε already produces a 5-node DT at 

ε = 10 −6 (see Fig. 6). The DT encodes the following rule: if station 1 is in its initial or transmitting phase ( s1 ≤ 1), let it finish if the bus is collision-free ( b ≤ 1, action end1 ) or trigger collision detection ( cd ) otherwise. If station 1 is backing off or done, schedule station 2 ( end2 ). At ε = 10 −2, the DT collapses to 3 nodes : the collision-handling branch disappears and the rule reduces to choosing end1 or end2 .

Israeli-Jalfon self-stabilisation ( n=10 ) [35, 40] is a self-stabilising algorithm with n processes. In the beginning, several processes have tokens. The scheduler chooses which process to activate in each step. However, it may only activate processes with tokens. After completion, the process randomly moves its token to its left or right neighbour, who deletes the token, if it already has one. The considered property states that there should finally be exactly one token. dtControl 2 

produces a 1317 -node DT and dtNESt a 719 -node DT. 

dtControl 2+ ε discovers a structural property of the pro-tocol: the probability of stabilisation is 1 for any scheduling strategy, so all scheduling choices are simultaneously optimal. Therefore, Dataset Construction produces an empty dataset. VII. C ONCLUSION AND FUTURE WORK 

dtControl 2+ ε combines the maturity of dtControl 2 

with the ability to trade the optimality of controllers for their compactness and explainability. Our methods for exploiting model knowledge and allowed imprecision ε enable our tool to produce controllers significantly smaller than the state of the art. Future work includes coupling our tool with SWITSS [41] or PET [54] to use witnessing subsystems or cores [48] for relevancy heuristics, as well as with dtPaynt [4] in the sense of [3] for advanced post-processing. Towards explainable con-trollers for LTL, we want to extend the API of stormpy and allow integration of semantic LTL-to-automaton translations as provided by e.g. Owl [49]. A. Linear Temporal Logic and Decision Trees 

We extend our approach to also represent ( ε-optimal) con-trollers for LTL queries. The major challenge is that LTL requires controllers with finite memory, such as the states of an ω-automaton for the LTL formula, see, e.g., [11, Chapter 10.6.4]. In essence, instead of simply yielding an action in each state, finite state controllers have a set of memory states 

M , and produce for each state-memory pair (s, m ) an action a,i.e. the action chosen in a state depends on the current memory state. Additionally, we output the underlying memory structure in form of an automaton. Using tight coupling with Storm ,

dtControl 2+ ε captures this information by extending the state with an additional variable that represents the current memory state. Thus, the learned DT is a mapping from MDP states and memory states to actions. We highlight that other tools like dtPaynt or dtNESt could theoretically represent such a mapping, too; however, Storm currently does not offer the option to export the mapping in this form, whereas our tool explicitly constructs it. Thus, dtControl 2+ ε is the only tool with LTL support. Still, due to technical limitations, the interpretability of DTs obtained this way is somewhat questionable. In particular, the memory states we can obtain through stormpy are opaque numbers without any further context. For example, the state representing “waiting for a request” may have the number 1, while “request received, preparing for send” may have the number 2, and so on. As such, a predicate of the form “automaton state ≤ 2” has no connection to the LTL formula and thus hardly any interpretable meaning. In particular, the numbering we obtain is arbitrary and may change from run to run. To tackle this issue, a connection to semantic 

automaton constructions (see [30]) implemented by tools such as Rabinizer [50] or Owl [49] would be required to obtain interpretable trees, which Storm does not (yet) support. We successfully evaluated our tool on several hand-crafted instances, confirming that dtControl 2+ ε can indeed con-struct DTs for strategies including memory. We omit LTL properties from our experimental evaluation because there are no established true LTL benchmarks on MDPs (i.e. more than constrained reachability), see [1, Section 5].

C. Incomparability of the Relevance Heuristics 

For Unreach (D), observe that whether a state is reachable or not under one particular controller is orthogonal to how many actions it has, whether it is dominated, whether its agency value is zero, or what the value of its actions are. For example, suppose we have two copies of an MDP and, in the initial state, we choose between them. Then one of the two copies will always be unreachable, yet whichever states satisfy one condition also satisfy it in the other copy. In particular, observe that all other methods do not depend on the controller or initial state. For AllOpt , consider the following example. Suppose in state s the single action goes to a state t or sink with probability 0.5 each. In t, we have two choices: one leads to the goal and the other to the sink. Here, s is not dominated (no other state is reached with probability 1 under every controller), and its agency equals 0.5 > 0. However, as there is only one available action, all available actions are optimal, and the state can be removed. When comparing Agency and Dominance , note that in a dominated state it is indeed relevant how the controller acts once the dominating (a.k.a. essential) state is reached. In contrast, Agency prescribes that all subsequent actions do not matter. For example, consider state s with a single action leading to state t, which in turn can choose between moving to the goal or a sink. The state s is dominated by t, but the agency of s equals 1. For the other way, consider a state with a single action that leads to a goal and sink each with probability 0.5. This state has no agency but is not dominated. We also note that AllOpt , Agency , and Dominance all imply EqVal . The former provides stronger guarantees, as these allow us to completely remove a state from the dataset instead of only adding the uniform action. However, EqVal 

indeed is a strictly weaker criterion, as exemplified in Fig. 3: The controller in which both states allow all actions is not optimal (since one can loop back and forth without reaching the goal), but the controller in which both play uniformly is optimal. 

D. Proof of Theorem 1 

For simplicity, we assume that, for all goal-based objectives (reachability, safety, and reachability cost), the goal/sink states are absorbing. (Note that most model checkers, in particu-lar Storm , perform this transformation automatically.) We formulate the proofs for reachability/safety, then outline the adaptation to reward-based objectives. Before we prove the overall correctness, we introduce some useful lemmas. 

Lemma 1: Let M = ( S, A, ¯s, δ ) be an MDP with reach-ability or safety objective G, s ∈ S a state, and π an ε-optimal controller. Let Aε ⊆ A(s) be all ε-optimal actions, i.e. |V (s, a ) − V (s)| ≤ ε for all a ∈ Aε. Then, randomly choosing among Aε is ε-optimal, i.e. replacing the choice of 

π in s with randomising over Aε in any way yields an ε-optimal controller. 

Proof. For now, we focus on the case of maximising reachabil-ity (equivalent to minimising safety). Let π′ be the controller obtained from π by randomising over Aε in s. Clearly, if s

is never visited, then the probability of reaching the goal is unchanged. Moreover, the probability of visiting s for the first time is the same under π and π′. Consider the set of paths that go through s and split them into those that then do not reach 

s again, and those that do. If they never visit s again, due to the Markov property, the value they achieve is exactly the one achieved by following π afterwards, i.e. ε-optimal. However, if they visit s again, we can split the paths again. Thus, by 14 Figure 7. Complete DT (left) and reduced DT (right) learnt for the optimal controller of the example in Section III. 

induction, we can prove for all paths that visit s finitely often that they achieve the same value as under π. Now, suppose that the set of paths that visit s infinitely often is not a zero measure set. Then, the probability of going back to s under 

π′ must be equal to 1. The same holds true for π, since until we come back to s, π and π′ behave the same. Consequently, the value of π′ is 0, meaning that V(s) ≤ ε and the choice in this state does not matter. (Note that, for this argument, it is important that π randomises over all actions in Aε, or at least has π′(s)( a) > 0 for all a with π(s)( a) > 0.) For maximising safety (and minimising reachability), the proof follows analogously. Notably, there we could even prove a slightly stronger statement, namely that π could randomise over any subset of Aε, as looping back to s infinitely often is optimal. □

reward), or, if not, we cannot obtain significantly more reward than π in between, as otherwise π would not be optimal. Observe that the controller π we started with only needs to be ε-optimal, and the controller we get by randomising is ε-optimal, too. Hence, we can apply Lemma 1 repeatedly to obtain that randomising in several states maintains ε-optimality.

Lemma 3: Permissive yields an optimal dataset, i.e. every controller consistent with the dataset is optimal. 

Proof. For safety objectives (minimizing reachability), choos-ing any action with optimal value in every state yields an optimal controller; this follows from, e.g., [29, Lemma 8] by inserting the value function for the upper bound U (note that that lemma even proves the claim for stochastic games, which generalize MDPs). Thus, it is correct that GoodActions 

picks all actions with optimal value. For reachability objectives, we additionally have to ensure progress, as exemplified in Example 1. For this, we first provide more detail on how the dataset is computed, i.e. on the GoodActions heuristic. Essentially, we restrict to the MDP where only locally optimal actions are allowed and then perform a backwards breadth-first search from the goal states, thus computing the minimum number of steps required to reach a goal state with positive probability. This is exactly what the distance operator described in [20, Section 3] does. Let r be the resulting distance function. Then, for each state 

s ∈ S, we add all actions a to the dataset that (i) are optimal, i.e. V(s) = V(s, a ), and that (ii) reduce the distance to the goal, i.e. there exists an s′ ∈ S such that δ(s, a, s ′) > 0 and 

r(s′) < r (s). For states with positive value, this set is non-empty, since they have a path to the target, and the distance function was computed based on only the optimal actions. Let π be a controller consistent with the dataset computed by GoodActions . This means that, for every state, there is an optimal action that reduces the distance to the goal with positive probability. Then, we apply [20, Proposition 5], using the value V as the lower bound x, the distance function 

r, and the controller π. Condition 1) of the proposition is satisfied because the controller picks actions reducing the distance; Condition 2) is satisfied as the controller picks optimal actions; Condition 3) is trivially satisfied since x = V.Thus, the proposition yields: Pπs [♢G] ≥ V(s). Since the value of a controller cannot be greater than the optimal value by definition, we conclude that for all states s ∈ S,we have Vπ (s) = Pπs [♢G]( s) = V(s). As we picked an arbitrary controller consistent with the dataset computed by 

GoodActions , we know that all controllers consistent with the dataset computed by GoodActions are optimal. 

□

Theorem 1: For every MDP and precision ε, every dataset 

D obtained using our initial dataset approaches and reduction procedure is ε-optimal, i.e. for every controller π consistent with D we have |V(¯ s) − Vπ (¯ s)| ≤ ε.

Proof. We split the proof into two parts, primarily referring to the lemmas above and combining the insights. First, we show that the initial dataset is optimal for all three possible choices. Second, we show that the reductions yield a dataset that is 

ε-optimal. For the initial dataset, Controller is optimal by definition, as it uses the actions of an optimal controller. Permissive 

is optimal by Lemma 3. Finally, for Combined , optimality follows from the fact that we effectively obtain a subset of 

Permissive , which is hence consistent with Permissive and thus optimal by Lemma 3. Finally, for all initial datasets, we also apply Unreach and 

EqVal . For Unreach , observe that removing unreachable states does not change the value of any consistent controller, as the induced probability measures are equal. To maintain this optimality argument when further modifying the dataset, we need to ensure that whenever we remove a state, we do not allow reaching what has been removed by Unreach and thereby make its choices matter. This restriction is part of how 16 we apply EqVal , making sure that we do not reach states that were removed by Unreach . The optimality of the uniform action added by EqVal follows from Lemma 1. Now for the reduction methods, our previous lemmas show that applying them to a safe dataset again yields a safe dataset, as follows. Correctness of Agency and Dominance follows from Lemma 2. Note that after applying these, the dataset is now only ε-optimal, since Agency utilises the allowed im-precision. For AllOpt , observe that if T (s) ∈ A(s) = D(s),the action is already safe by assumption. If instead T (s)

yields uniform choice (by virtue of s not being defined by T ), correctness of this choice follows from Lemma 1. In terms of “escaping” the reachable set, observe that as soon as we reach a state satisfying Agency , no subsequent actions matter. For 

Dominance , if s is reachable, then s′ is always reached, no matter what we choose in s. Thus, s′ must be reachable as well. Similarly, for AllOpt , if s is reachable and all of its actions are in the dataset, then none of its successors could have been removed by Unreach . □
